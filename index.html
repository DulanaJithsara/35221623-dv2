RSPCA animal welfare in Australia from 2019–2025

Through the Shelter Door

Fewer cats and dogs are arriving at the Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (RSPCA) than five years ago. Yet the animals that do arrive are harder to help, wildlife now makes up more than a third of the work, and more cruelty cases are ending up in court.

By Dulana Jithsara Sarathchandra · October 2026 - Data: RSPCA Australia National Statistics 2024–25 and the Australian Bureau of Statistics

01 / 06

Fewer animals are coming through the door

In 2020–21 the RSPCA took in more than 103,000 animals. Four years later, the number was 77,464.

1 / 2

Cats fell fastest

Cat intake fell from 42,572 to 25,639, a drop of 40%. Dogs fell by 29%. Wildlife and other animals barely changed.

(Hover over any point to see the exact number for that year.)

2 / 2

90% of the drop came from dogs and cats

Of the 25,593 fewer animals, 16,933 were cats and 6,459 were dogs.

The RSPCA notes that intake depends partly on how many council pound contracts each state society runs, so a fall does not simply mean that fewer animals need help.

A smaller shelter population does not mean an easier job, as the next chapter shows.

02 / 06

Who arrives, and what happens next

Most people picture dogs and cats when they think of the RSPCA. In 2024–25, the biggest group was native wildlife.

1 / 3

Wildlife is the largest group, and almost all of it is in Queensland

Of the 27,794 wild animals received nationally, 26,564 (96%) came through RSPCA Queensland.

Each rectangle's area is proportional to the number of animals. Hover over one to see the exact count.

2 / 3

Two very different journeys

Most cats and dogs leave alive: 64% of cats and 43% of dogs are adopted by a new family, and a quarter of dogs go home to their owner.

Wild animals cannot be adopted and therefore the best outcome is to release back to the wild, which happened for only 9%. Majority (65%) were euthanased.

3 / 3

Dogs are the exception: a rising euthanasia rate

Over four years, the share of cats and wild animals that were euthanased stayed almost flat. For dogs it rose from 11.2% to 17.5%.

Fewer dogs now arrive, which suggests that those that do arrive are more likely to have serious medical or behavioural problems. Chapter 4 looks at the reasons.

03 / 06

Where in Australia

Raw totals make the biggest states look busiest. Adjusting for population, and showing what kind of animals arrive, gives a fairer picture.

1 / 3

Queensland stands out, and not only because of wildlife

RSPCA Queensland received 75.9 animals per 10,000 residents, nearly three times the national rate of 28.

Use the menu to switch the category of animals: Queensland is still among the highest, alongside the ACT.

2 / 3

The mix of animals differs from state to state

Each dot stands for 50 animals. Queensland is crowded with green wildlife dots, while cats dominate in New South Wales, Victoria and South Australia.

3 / 3

South Australia was the only state to grow

Intakes fell in seven of the eight state and territory RSPCAs last year, while South Australia rose by 13%. Tasmania's small fall hides a big shift within it where the dog intake almost tripled, from 130 to 379.

Note: RSPCA ACT, NT and Tasmania do not include council pound animals in their figures, while the other states do.

04 / 06

Going home, or not

A lost dog has a fair chance of finding its way back to its owner. A lost cat almost never does.

1 / 2

Dogs get reclaimed; cats get adopted

Nationally, 24.8% of dogs were reunited with their owner, compared with just 5.3% of cats. Cats that leave alive almost always need a brand-new home.

Making sure a cat is microchipped, with up-to-date contact details, is one of the simplest ways owners can change this.

2 / 2

Why animals are euthanased

For dogs, behaviour is the most common reason: 1,652 of 2,768 dogs (60%) were euthanased because they were judged unsafe. For cats, illness and injury come first (47%).

Brighter cells mark the main reason in each state. The number under each state is how many animals its column covers, so be careful with small columns such as NT or Tasmania.

05 / 06

Cruelty complaints and court cases

RSPCA inspectors investigate reports of cruelty and neglect. In most states they can also take offenders to court.

1 / 3

Tasmania and South Australia report the most per person

RSPCA NSW and Queensland investigated the most complaints. Per person, though, Tasmania (33 per 10,000 residents) and South Australia (27) were the busiest.

The Northern Territory has no RSPCA inspectorate; animal welfare laws there are enforced by the NT Government.

2 / 3

Fewer complaints, more prosecutions

In 2024–25 complaints fell to a six-year low of 67,237, yet finalised prosecutions jumped 54%, from 248 to 381.

These national complaint figures count repeat visits to the same case, which is why they are higher than the investigations shown on the map.

3 / 3

NSW drives the national increase

RSPCA NSW finalised 226 of the 381 prosecutions nationally. Victoria and Western Australia had the highest success rates (47 of 47 and 59 of 61).

06 / 06

How the RSPCA's priorities are changing

Put together, the numbers show an organisation shifting from handling large volumes of pets towards fewer, more complex cases.

−36%

Harder pet cases

Dog and cat intake has fallen since 2020–21, but a greater share of dogs are now euthanased, most often for behavioural reasons.

96%

Wildlife on the front line

More than a third of all animals are native wildlife, and 96% of them are cared for in Queensland.

+54%

More enforcement

Complaints were at a six-year low in 2024–25, yet finalised prosecutions rose sharply.

What you can do

  • Microchip and register your pets, and keep your contact details up to date.
  • Desex cats and dogs to reduce unwanted litters.
  • Consider adopting from a shelter: cats are the most likely animals to need a new home.
  • Report suspected cruelty to your state RSPCA or, in the NT, the NT Government. How to report
